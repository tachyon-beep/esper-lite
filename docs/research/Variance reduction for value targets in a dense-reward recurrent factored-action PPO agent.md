# Variance reduction for value targets in a dense-reward recurrent factored-action PPO agent

## Executive verdict

**Bottom line.** It is **highly likely** that your high-CoV counterfactual contribution term is making the critic’s regression problem harder, and that moving it **out of the value-function regressand** will improve value-target stability and your explained-variance metric. But the move is only **objective-preserving** under a stricter condition than your hypothesis assumes: removing that term from reward is safe **only if** the policy objective you actually care about is already fully represented by the sparse terminal accuracy minus costs, plus shaping terms that are genuinely potential-based. Potential-based shaping preserves the optimal policy of the **base** reward; it does **not** license dropping an arbitrary extra reward term and claiming the optimum is unchanged. Ng, Harada, and Russell proved that potential-based shaping is the class that preserves optimal policies in the standard setting, and Devlin and Kudenko extended this to dynamic potentials; Harutyunyan et al. then showed how an arbitrary advice reward can be translated into a dynamic potential-based auxiliary process that captures that advice “in expectation”, but that is not the same thing as saying the original advice reward itself can simply be deleted without changing the task. citeturn30search21turn11view1turn41view0turn41view2

So the central hypothesis is **sound in a qualified form**: **use the counterfactual contribution as a control variate, auxiliary target, or separate critic stream rather than as part of the critic’s main return target**, **provided** your true optimisation target is the sparse task outcome plus costs and the remaining dense terms are policy-invariant shaping. If, instead, the contribution term is part of what defines “good behaviour” beyond terminal accuracy and cost, then removing it from reward changes the objective, and PBRS does not rescue that. citeturn30search21turn14view0turn41view1

On the separate critic issue: **de-shaping is complementary to, not a substitute for, fixing your action-conditioned baseline.** Your current critic is estimating \(Q(s,\text{op})\) and then using a single sampled \(Q(s',\text{op}')\) both as baseline and bootstrap. That differs from the standard PPO/GAE setup, where the baseline is state-dependent and the bootstrap is a value estimate. Recent theory and diagnostics around baselines matter here: Tucker et al. showed that learned action-dependent baselines often do **not** reduce variance as much as advertised in practice, and that gains were often driven by implementation quirks or weak state-value fitting; Mei et al. further showed that baselines can improve optimisation for reasons beyond variance reduction alone. In your setting, the small discrete operation head makes an exact marginal \(V(s)=\sum_{\text{op}}\pi(\text{op}\mid s)Q(s,\text{op})\) cheap enough that there is little literature support for keeping the sampled \(Q(s',\text{op}')\) bootstrap as the main value target. citeturn42view0turn6academia9turn37search0turn37search1

## How to use the counterfactual signal without poisoning the critic target

The modern, correct way to use a hand-built high-variance signal \(c_t\) as a control variate is to treat it like a **baseline term with its expectation added back**, not like a reward term whose realised value directly enters the critic target. In policy-gradient form, the safe pattern is to weight the score term by a task advantage and subtract a zero-mean control variate, for example \(A^{\text{task}}_t-\alpha(c_t-\mu_t)\), where \(\mu_t\) estimates the conditional expectation of \(c_t\) given the information the baseline is allowed to use. This preserves unbiasedness if the baseline term has zero expected score, which is automatic for state-dependent baselines and extends to richer baselines when their conditioning is chosen correctly. Classical control-variate reasoning and modern PPO-oriented extensions both support this view, including coordinate-wise and layer-wise control variates and input-dependent baselines when exogenous randomness is a major source of variance. citeturn40view0turn23view0

The catch, which matters a lot for your contribution signal, is that **raw counterfactual contribution is action-related by construction**. If you simply subtract it from a policy-gradient estimator as though it were state-only, you risk bias. This is why the action-dependent-baseline literature is relevant but also cautionary. Wu et al. derived **bias-free action-dependent factorised baselines** for structured policies and showed empirical gains in high-dimensional control and hand-manipulation domains. But Tucker et al. then showed that much of the claimed empirical superiority of learned state-action baselines disappeared against properly fit state baselines, and that fitting a powerful action-dependent baseline on the current batch can itself bias gradients through overfitting. In other words, an action-dependent home for your signal exists in principle, but the literature strongly suggests doing it **carefully, lagged, and factor-aware**, not by plugging the realised contribution straight into PPO’s critic target or same-batch advantage estimator. citeturn6academia11turn42view0

This is where your **factored action space and causal masks** are actually an advantage. For policies with structured factorisation, the best-supported form is not a single blunt \(Q(s,a)\) baseline, but a baseline that respects which action factors can causally affect which downstream targets. Spooner et al. frame this with an action-target influence network and a **factor baseline**; they show that factorised control variates generalise action-dependent baselines and counterfactual policy gradients, and that variance reduction depends on correctly specifying the influence structure. That maps unusually well to your operation head plus conditionally active sub-heads. In your case, if the contribution signal is used as a per-head control variate, it should be conditioned only on the parents allowed by the causal mask, with inactive heads excluded by construction. citeturn26view0

The literature closest to your “dense counterfactual signal as baseline, not reward” idea is the hindsight/counterfactual-baseline line. Hindsight Credit Assignment and Counterfactual Credit Assignment both use future-conditioned value functions to separate “skill” from “luck”, and Mesnard et al. explicitly formulate policy-gradient algorithms that use future-conditional value functions **as baselines or critics**, with provably lower variance when hindsight information is constrained so that it is independent of the current action. Posterior Value Functions go in the same direction: they prove that hindsight baselines never increase variance and can reduce it relative to ordinary state values. For a recurrent PPO agent, the natural instantiation is a backward-looking auxiliary network or backward LSTM that predicts the expected counterfactual signal from future trajectory information **while adversarially purging information about the current action** before it touches the baseline. That is the principled home for your leave-one-out contribution signal. citeturn36view0turn8view0

What does **not** transfer cleanly are Stein-style action-dependent control variates. Liu et al.’s Stein control-variate method is elegant and empirically effective, but it relies on a **reparameterisable** policy and action-derivative machinery that fit continuous-control settings much better than a discrete operation head with masked categorical sub-heads. For your setup, Wu-style factor baselines and CCA-style hindsight baselines are much more natural than Stein control variates. citeturn43search0turn43search5turn6academia11

## What PBRS and reward decomposition buy you

For the part of your reward that **really is shaping**, the literature is clear. Under potential-based shaping, the optimal action ordering is preserved because the shaped value function is just the original value shifted by a state-only potential. Recent PBRS work still states this in exactly that form: \(Q^*_{M'}(s,a)=Q^*_M(s,a)-\Phi(s)\), so the **advantage** is unchanged even though the critic’s absolute target is shifted. This is the right formal lens for your seed-lifecycle potential table and any accuracy potential that is genuinely of the form \(\gamma\Phi(s')-\Phi(s)\). citeturn14view0

That said, “policy invariance” is weaker than “easy critic learning”. Shaping changes the value function that the critic must fit. Sometimes that makes learning easier by reducing delay and aligning the target with observable progress; sometimes it makes it harder by injecting sharp jumps, poor scaling, or hidden state dependence. Müller and Kudenko’s 2025 analysis is useful here: they emphasise that although PBRS preserves the optimal policy and policy ordering, its practical effectiveness depends on the interaction between the potential, the initial values, and the external reward scale, and they show limitations of continuous potentials in assigning clean positive and negative shaping signals. This is directly relevant to your concern that even “invariant” shaping can still destabilise EV if it creates a harder regression target. citeturn15view0turn15view1turn15view2

Harutyunyan et al. are especially relevant to your case because they address the exact bridge from “arbitrary hand-built reward-like advice” to “optimum-preserving shaping”. Their method learns a **secondary value function** for an arbitrary reward function and uses its estimates as **dynamic advice potentials**, so that the resulting shaping reward captures the advice function **in expectation** while remaining within the PBRS framework. The important implication for you is subtle: if your counterfactual contribution is advice about where reward should go, the literature-supported way to keep its behavioural guidance **without redefining the task objective** is not to leave it as dense reward, but to **translate it into a dynamic shaping process or auxiliary potential**, or to use it as a baseline target. That is much closer to your intended move than plain reward deletion. The downside is that empirical validation for dynamic advice potentials remains comparatively small-scale compared with PPO-on-Procgen or large-control benchmarks. citeturn41view0turn41view1turn41view2

Reward decomposition adds another useful tool. Hybrid Reward Architecture learns separate value functions for semantically decomposed reward channels and showed strong gains on Ms. Pac-Man because each sub-value function depends on a smaller feature subset than the full scalar reward. Later work on reward decomposition and reward-distribution modelling pushes the same core idea: where reward channels differ in scale, smoothness, or uncertainty, a single critic on the sum can be harder to fit than multiple critics on the components. For your setup, that means a separate head for the high-CoV contribution stream is **very likely** to stabilise the **main** value head that regresses on terminal accuracy, rent, and policy-invariant shaping. But there is a measurement trap: if you continue to report EV only on the **total summed return**, the noisy contribution head can still dominate the aggregate EV. The clean way to tell whether the redesign helps is to track **EV\_main**, **EV\_cf**, and **EV\_sum** separately. citeturn9search4turn9search1turn27view0

Your “escrow” variant deserves a hard warning. If you clip a potential **difference** after computing it, you break telescoping and lose the PBRS guarantee. The literature is unambiguous that policy invariance comes from the telescoping structure itself; once clipping binds on the delta, you no longer have a pure potential-based transformation. If you need bounded shaping magnitude, the safer move is to define a **bounded potential** first and then difference that bounded potential, or to move the shaped quantity out of reward and into an auxiliary stream. Feeding the potential state, escrow state, or prior unrealised-credit state into the observation is also a recognised way to recover Markov structure when rewards or transitions depend on hidden internal variables: state augmentation is a standard response to delayed or history-dependent dynamics, and recent analyses of delayed RL explicitly show that such augmentation can transform a non-Markov process back into an equivalent MDP. citeturn30search21turn11view1turn33search0turn31search13

## PPO-specific and recurrent considerations

For PPO specifically, it is important not to over-credit normalisation for solving this problem. PopArt-style normalisation helps because reward scale, reward sparsity, and agent competence all change the magnitude of actor-critic updates; Hessel et al. showed that PopArt can make actor-critic updates invariant to those factors and materially improve performance in sparse/dense multi-task settings. But your current scheme is explicitly **not full PopArt**: van Hasselt et al.’s PopArt is “Preserving Outputs Precisely, while Adaptively Rescaling Targets”, and the output-preserving rescale of the final layer is the point. A running mean/std without output-preserving compensation reduces some scale issues but still changes the effective target as the statistics move. So even before touching reward design, a full PopArt implementation is likely to make the value target more stationary than your current “PopArt-lite”; it just will not remove variance created by a fundamentally noisy contribution channel or by a sampled action-conditioned bootstrap. citeturn16search3turn18view0

The recurrent side matters as well. R2D2’s main practical findings were that **representation drift** and **recurrent-state staleness** matter, and that **burn-in** helps; Kapturowski et al. used both to mitigate stale hidden-state problems. Pleines et al. make a parallel point for recurrent PPO: sequence arrangement, correct initial hidden states for sequence starts, and masking of padded steps are not optional details. In your case, the “anchored reference pass” is sensible for keeping the PPO ratio reference fixed across inner epochs, but it does not by itself solve value-target variance if the critic is still bootstrapping with a behaviour-sampled \(Q(s',\text{op}')\) and learning from a regressor whose hidden state or normalisation statistics move during the \(K\) epochs. That is one reason your EV instability is plausibly coming from **both** reward noise and critic-target construction. citeturn18view3turn17search1

There is also a direct practical implication from Tucker et al. for your current architecture. They found that one of the biggest real-world wins came not from making the baseline action-dependent, but from **improving the value-function parameterisation** itself, including simple fixes like horizon-awareness. For you, the analogous move is to stop treating a sampled \(Q(s',\text{op}')\) as the main bootstrap and instead learn a proper \(V(s)\), or at least the exact op-marginal \(V(s)=\sum_{\text{op}}\pi(\text{op}\mid s)Q(s,\text{op})\). Because your operation head is discrete and small, exact marginalisation is cheap. That change attacks the critic problem at its source. De-shaping then reduces the variance of the target that this better critic is asked to fit. citeturn42view0turn37search0turn37search1

The critic-free alternatives are real, but the evidence base is narrower for your kind of environment. RLOO and GRPO replace the learned value baseline with a within-group empirical baseline from multiple rollouts of the same prompt or context, and recent RLHF/RLVR work shows that this can outperform PPO while using much less memory in LLM training. However, that literature also already shows failure modes: GRPO’s optimisation can be biased, leading to variants like Dr. GRPO, and empirical-mean group baselines can themselves be noisy under limited rollout budgets, motivating shrinkage baselines such as James–Stein. The deeper mismatch for your setup is structural: these methods need **multiple comparable rollouts from the same conditioning context**. If your environment can cheaply fork the host-training state and replay common random numbers, then a grouped leave-one-out baseline is viable and elegant. If not, the method becomes much less attractive than a good \(V(s)\) critic with auxiliary counterfactual streams. citeturn20search1turn20search7turn20search17turn21search15turn24view0turn22search0

## Ranked recommendations for your agent

**First choice: replace the main critic with an op-independent value baseline, and de-shape the regressand to the low-variance task stream.** Concretely, make the critic regress on terminal validation accuracy plus rent plus only those dense terms that are provably PBRS, while learning either a separate \(V(s)\) head or an exact op-marginal \(V(s)=\sum_{\text{op}}\pi(\text{op}\mid s)Q(s,\text{op})\). This directly addresses the two strongest variance sources in your current setup: the noisy contribution term in the target, and the sampled action-conditioned bootstrap. Assumptions required: that the task objective you truly care about is terminal accuracy/cost plus PBRS; that holds if the counterfactual contribution is advisory rather than constitutive. Expected effect: **large** reduction in value-target variance and a materially stabler EV. Implementation cost: **moderate**, because you already have the ingredients and your op head is small. citeturn30search21turn14view0turn42view0turn37search0

**Second choice: keep the counterfactual contribution as an auxiliary stream or control variate, not as dense reward.** The safest implementation is a separate head that predicts the expected contribution signal from the recurrent state, trained as an auxiliary task, and optionally used in the actor update as a centred control variate \(c_t-\hat{\mu}_t\) with stop-gradient. If you want to go further, use a CCA-style future-conditional baseline with a backward recurrent encoder and an action-independence constraint, so the auxiliary stream captures “skill vs luck” without leaking the chosen action into the baseline. Assumptions required: either state-only conditional expectation for unbiasedness, or a properly constructed action-independent hindsight representation. That partially holds for you because the environment is recurrent and you already have delayed noisy outcomes; it does **not** hold if you use the raw realised contribution as the baseline. Expected effect: **moderate to large** reduction in actor-gradient variance and retention of credit-assignment information, with less pollution of the value target. Implementation cost: **moderate to high**. citeturn36view0turn8view0turn40view0

**Third choice: use factor-aware baselines aligned with your causal masks.** Instead of one baseline for the whole factored action, learn per-factor or per-head baselines that only condition on the action factors allowed by an influence structure, and only for heads that were causally active. This is the literature-supported way to exploit the structure you already know your action space has. Assumptions required: that your causal mask is approximately correct and stable enough to define the influence map; that appears to hold in your environment by design. Expected effect: **moderate** variance reduction, especially for sparse sub-heads that are currently dominated by the always-active op head in global standardisation. Implementation cost: **moderate**. citeturn26view0turn6academia11

**Fourth choice: if you must keep advisory dense guidance in the objective, convert it into dynamic potential-based shaping rather than direct reward.** This is the Harutyunyan-style route: learn a secondary value function for the advice signal and use it as a dynamic advice potential, which captures the advice in expectation while remaining in the PBRS family. This is the only literature-backed route that comes close to “keep the credit-assignment value without changing the optimum” for an arbitrary hand-built signal. Assumptions required: episodic setting, careful dynamic-potential construction, and acceptance of evidence that is theoretically attractive but empirically narrower than modern PPO benchmarks. Expected effect: **small to moderate** target-variance improvement versus dense direct reward, while preserving behavioural guidance. Implementation cost: **high** relative to a simple auxiliary head. citeturn41view0turn41view1turn11view1

**Fifth choice: consider grouped leave-one-out baselines only if you can cheaply generate paired rollouts from the same host state with common random numbers.** If you can fork the host-training process and evaluate several policy samples from the same recurrent history, then an RLOO-style baseline gives you a critic-free control variate and avoids the \(Q(s,\text{op})\) problem entirely. But if you cannot create these matched groups cheaply and reproducibly, the method loses most of its appeal outside LLM-style sequence-level RL. Assumptions required: efficient state cloning, grouped rollouts, and enough group size to keep empirical-baseline variance acceptable. Expected effect: potentially **large** variance reduction when the grouping is valid; otherwise little benefit. Implementation cost: **very high** unless the simulator already supports exact branching. citeturn20search1turn20search17turn24view0turn22search0

The main traps are easy to state. **First**, de-shaping can silently change the optimum if the removed counterfactual term was part of the task rather than mere advice; PBRS preserves the optimum of the base task, not the optimum after deleting a non-PBRS term. **Second**, baselines do not provide exploration incentives the way dense rewards do, so moving the contribution signal out of reward may slow learning unless the remaining PBRS is strong enough or the auxiliary baseline is very informative. **Third**, same-batch fitting of powerful action-dependent baselines can bias gradients or wash out the learning signal. **Fourth**, clipping a potential difference breaks telescoping. **Fifth**, your current PopArt-lite normaliser may itself contribute avoidable target non-stationarity because it omits PopArt’s output-preserving rescale. citeturn30search21turn15view1turn42view0turn16search3turn18view0

My overall recommendation is therefore:

Use a **proper \(V(s)\) baseline** for PPO/GAE, preferably the exact op-marginal given your small discrete operation space;  
train the critic on a **de-shaped low-variance return** consisting of sparse terminal outcome, rent, and only strict PBRS terms;  
keep the counterfactual contribution in a **separate auxiliary stream**, and, if you want it to affect the actor update, use it as a **centred control variate or hindsight baseline**, not as part of the main critic target;  
track **per-stream EV** rather than only EV on the total return;  
and eliminate any **delta clipping** on purported PBRS terms unless you re-express the bound as a bounded potential before differencing. The literature fit for that package is much stronger than either “leave everything dense in reward” or “go fully critic-free with GRPO/RLOO” in your recurrent, factored-action PPO setting. citeturn36view0turn26view0turn14view0turn42view0turn18view0
