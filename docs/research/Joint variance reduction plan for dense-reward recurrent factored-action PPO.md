# Joint variance reduction plan for dense-reward recurrent factored-action PPO

## Executive verdict

The two fixes are **complementary, not substitutes**. Fixing the action-conditioned baseline and the single-sample bootstrap addresses a *structural estimator problem* in PPO/GAE: your current critic injects action-dependent cancellation into the policy signal and adds avoidable bootstrap variance by sampling one next operation instead of taking an expectation. De-shaping the value target addresses a *target-noise problem*: even a correct value baseline will still be volatile if it is trained on a return dominated by a high-coefficient-of-variation counterfactual contribution term. On the evidence available, the best sequence is: **first remove the cheapest structural variance source by switching to an action-independent baseline with expected/all-action bootstrapping; then de-shape the main value target and move the high-CoV counterfactual term into a separate auxiliary stream; then, if sparse sub-heads still learn poorly, add COMA-style per-head counterfactual baselines for the causally active heads only.** citeturn38view0turn38view3turn18view8turn19view1turn39view0turn21view9turn18view4

A concise recommendation follows. Use a **true recurrent \(V(s)\) head** as the only PPO baseline and GAE bootstrap. Keep \(Q(s,\mathrm{op})\) only as an auxiliary/telemetry head. During rollout processing, replace the sampled next-operation bootstrap with either the learned \(V(s')\) directly or, during a transition period, the frozen-policy marginal \(V_{\text{marg}}(s')=\sum_{\mathrm{op}}\pi_{\text{old}}(\mathrm{op}\mid s')Q(s',\mathrm{op})\), which is a proper control variate and the discrete-action analogue of Expected Sarsa / expected policy gradients. Then train that \(V\) head on a **de-shaped low-variance return** consisting of exact PBRS deltas, terminal accuracy, rent, and other small Markovian costs, while moving the leave-one-out/Shapley-style contribution term into its own auxiliary prediction stream with its own normaliser. Only after those two steps, consider **per-head counterfactual baselines** for sparse heads. Confidence in this sequence is **high** for the first step, **medium-high** for the second, and **medium** for the third. citeturn38view0turn18view8turn19view1turn20view3turn21view11turn24view1turn30view1turn21view9

## What the literature implies for your reward-target problem

Your intuition about the reward side is broadly right, but there is an important correction. A hand-built high-variance signal is **not automatically a valid policy-gradient control variate** just because it is informative. For unbiased score-function variance reduction, the subtracted term must have zero conditional contribution under the relevant action distribution; in practice that means a baseline independent of the sampled action, or an action-dependent baseline built in a way that preserves the zero-expectation condition. Q-Prop used a critic-derived control variate via a Taylor expansion, and Stein-style work generalised action-dependent control variates, but Tucker et al. showed that learned action-dependent baselines often failed to reduce variance in the commonly used domains once implementation artefacts were removed. More recent work after 2021 moved more towards vector-valued or coordinate-wise control variates in PPO, and there is some renewed off-policy theory on optimal action-dependent baselines, but there is **no strong modern empirical consensus** that “plug in a learned action-dependent baseline” is a dependable variance cure in on-policy deep actor-critic. Confidence here is **high**. citeturn20view0turn9search2turn39view0turn21view2turn21view3turn29view0

That matters directly for your leave-one-out contribution term. As a **reward component**, it inflates the critic’s regressand variance. As a **raw actor baseline**, it is risky unless you can subtract its **conditional expectation under the current factor’s action distribution** and guarantee that the residual baseline term does not leak the chosen action in a way that biases the gradient. Mesnard et al.’s counterfactual credit-assignment work is the most principled modern reference point here: it reduces variance by conditioning value functions on carefully chosen future information, while explicitly constraining hindsight information so that it does not contain the agent’s action information in a way that breaks unbiasedness. For your setup, that literature supports the claim that the leave-one-out signal has a more principled home as a **separate auxiliary target or future-conditional side prediction** than as a raw reward fed into the main critic, unless you construct a proper conditional-expectation baseline around it. Confidence is **medium-high**, because the theory is strong but direct deep PPO evidence in your exact setting is limited. citeturn18view0turn20view0turn39view0

Reward decomposition is one of the clearest fits to your critic-noise story. Hybrid Reward Architecture learned a separate value function for each reward component and argued that each component can be easier to approximate because it depends on a smaller or simpler subset of features. RD2 extended that basic idea with learned disentanglement and showed reward decomposition can improve sample efficiency, albeit mostly in value-based settings. The direct actor-critic evidence is thinner, but the premise matches your case unusually well: one dense component has very different variance and semantics from the others. A dedicated head for the high-CoV counterfactual term is therefore more plausible than a single critic on the summed reward. The expected effect is not that the auxiliary head magically fixes the actor, but that it **stops the main EV metric from being dominated by one noisy component**. Confidence is **medium** because the fit is conceptually strong but most evidence is not from recurrent PPO. citeturn20view3turn21view11turn18view2

On PBRS specifically, the literature supports your “de-shape the target” idea **only under the exact PBRS assumptions**. Ng, Harada, and Russell showed that potential-based shaping preserves the optimal policy, and later work extended this to dynamic potentials. More recent work continues to emphasise that PBRS preserves the optimal-policy ordering, but also that learnability depends on the chosen potential and the surrounding value scales. A useful modern statement appears in Bootstrapped Reward Shaping: for PBRS, the shaped task has the same optimal policy, and the shaped value functions shift predictably by the potential term; the paper explicitly notes the telescoping cancellation and gives the shaped-value relation. The practical implication for your EV target is subtle but important: PBRS can make behaviour learning easier **while making the critic learn a different value surface**, namely the base value shifted by the potential. That is usually fine, but only if the potential term is itself Markovian and properly represented to the critic. Confidence is **high**. citeturn12search0turn24view0turn24view1turn21view4turn21view5turn30view1

Your escrow variant is the main caveat. Once you clip a  difference when the clip binds, the telescoping/property-preserving argument no longer holds as stated. The literature on non-Markovian rewards gives the standard remedy: if reward depends on a hidden accumulator, automaton state, or history summary, augment the state with that summary so the reward becomes Markov again. In your case that means exposing the relevant escrow state — such as the min-over-window stable-accuracy statistic, prior potential, or any clip accumulator state — to the critic, rather than asking the LSTM to infer it from raw observation history alone. If you do not do that, you have introduced a partially observed reward process on top of an already recurrent policy, which tends to worsen bootstrap noise and critic fit. Confidence is **medium-high**. citeturn32view0turn32view1

Normalisation does help, but it does not solve either of your core problems. PopArt was introduced precisely because value-target magnitudes can move over time, and the full PopArt mechanism preserves the *unnormalised* value output when the running statistics change by rescaling the last layer. MAPPO later found that value normalisation “never hurts” and often improves final performance, and can reduce training variance. However, a “PopArt-lite” scheme that tracks running moments without the output-preserving rescale does **not** remove the non-stationarity induced by stat changes in the same way. So your current normalisation stack is useful, but it is not a substitute for fixing the regressand and the baseline design. Confidence is **high**. citeturn37view1turn37view4turn26view3

## What the literature implies for your action-conditioned baseline problem

The baseline problem is the more urgent one because it mixes **correctness-like concerns** with variance concerns. Classical policy-gradient theory says that subtracting a state baseline is unbiased, and \(V(s)=\sum_a \pi(a\mid s)Q(s,a)\) is the canonical choice that turns \(Q\) into an advantage. Expected Sarsa then showed, in the TD setting, that replacing the sampled next action with its policy expectation lowers update variance while preserving convergence under the same conditions as Sarsa; it also identified \(V(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)Q(s,a)\) explicitly as the expected next-state value. For discrete actions, Mean Actor-Critic and Expected Policy Gradients extended the same idea to policy gradients: using all action values rather than only the executed action reduces variance. Because you already compute all operations in one batched forward pass, this is as close to a free lunch as the literature offers. Confidence is **high**. citeturn38view0turn38view3turn19view1turn18view8

By contrast, using \(Q(s,\mathrm{op_{taken}})\) itself as the baseline for the operation head is a poor fit to what the literature says. Tucker et al. found that learned action-dependent baselines often do not reduce variance relative to ordinary state baselines in common domains once implementation details are controlled. In your setup the problem is even sharper: the same \(Q\) is being asked to serve three roles at once — state evaluation, action scoring, and bootstrap target — and those roles conflict. Subtracting \(Q(s,\mathrm{op_{taken}})\) from an update for the very same op term invites exactly the partial cancellation you described. Theoretical action-dependent-baseline papers show that carefully constructed factorised action baselines can be unbiased and sometimes helpful in high-dimensional actions, but that is not the same as using a sampled \(Q(s,a)\) head as *both* baseline and bootstrap in PPO/GAE. Confidence is **high** that your present design is more likely to hurt than to help. citeturn39view0turn21view0turn21view1

The strongest literature analogue for your factored heads is indeed cooperative multi-agent RL. COMA’s core idea is to compute an agent-specific advantage by marginalising out *one* agent’s action while holding the others fixed, using a centralised critic that can evaluate all those counterfactuals in one pass. COMA explicitly motivates this as a counterfactual baseline related to aristocrat utility / difference rewards, and shows that its counterfactual baseline is crucial when using a central \(Q\)-critic. Li et al.’s Difference Advantage Estimation then brought a GAE-like exponentially weighted estimator to the multi-agent credit-assignment setting and reported substantial empirical gains over other estimators. This is the best available prior for your causally masked sub-heads. The transfer is not exact — your heads are not independent agents, and some sub-actions are continuous-ish schedule parameters — but the structural analogy is real. Confidence is **medium** for transfer, **high** for the analogy. citeturn21view9turn21view10turn18view4turn28search16

That leads to a practical distinction between two candidate baselines. Using the **marginal value** \(V_{\text{marg}}(s)=\sum_{\mathrm{op}}\pi(\mathrm{op}\mid s)Q(s,\mathrm{op})\) is theoretically correct as a control variate for the operation factor and immediately removes the single-sample next-op variance if used in the bootstrap. But learning a **separate \(V(s)\) head directly** is usually the cleaner long-term design, because \(V_{\text{marg}}\) is coupled to a moving policy and to the \(Q\) head’s own estimation noise. In PPO with multiple inner epochs, that coupling is especially awkward unless the expectation is taken under the **frozen rollout/reference policy**, not the continually updated current policy. For your anchored-reference recurrent PPO, the safest version is to compute any expected bootstrap from the rollout-time or anchored-reference op distribution, so the target stays fixed across PPO epochs. Confidence is **high** on the frozen-policy expectation point, and **medium-high** that a direct \(V\) head will be more stable than a marginal-\(Q\) critic over time. citeturn38view0turn18view8turn19view1turn39view0

Keeping \(Q(s,\mathrm{op})\) as an auxiliary head is reasonable. The literature on auxiliary or decomposed value heads does not show that a side \(Q\) head is inherently destabilising, but recurrent-POMDP work does warn that shared recurrent actor-critic trunks can be brittle and that unsharing actor and critic recurrence can materially help. That means the right pattern for you is: let \(V(s)\) be the only head that touches PPO/GAE, keep \(Q(s,\mathrm{op})\) for telemetry, diagnostics, or optional distillation, and either down-weight its loss or move it to a partially separate critic branch if it injects noise into the shared recurrent representation. Confidence is **medium-high**. citeturn20view3turn34search4turn18view7

## The ranked sequence of changes

The best fit to your constraints is a short ranked sequence rather than a menu.

**Use an action-independent value baseline and expected next-op bootstrap immediately.**  
Core idea: stop using \(Q(s,\mathrm{op_{taken}})\) as the PPO baseline and GAE bootstrap. Replace it with either a direct \(V(s)\) head or, as a transitional first patch, the frozen-policy marginal \(V_{\text{marg}}(s)=\sum_{\mathrm{op}}\pi_{\text{old}}(\mathrm{op}\mid s)Q(s,\mathrm{op})\). Replace the sampled bootstrap \(Q(s',\mathrm{op}'\sim\beta)\) with the full expectation over operations. This is the best cost-adjusted change because Expected Sarsa, EPG, and MAC all argue for lower variance when expectations replace sampled next-actions, and your all-ops critic forward is already cheap. Required assumptions: discrete op set with cheap enumeration; rollout/reference op probabilities available; action-independent state value is meaningful. Those assumptions **hold** in your setup. Expected effect: **medium-to-large reduction** in target variance and a likely immediate improvement in EV stability, especially for the operation head. Implementation cost: **low**. Evidence strength: **strong theory, solid empirical support in related discrete-action settings, but not directly on recurrent factored PPO**. Confidence: **very likely** beneficial. citeturn38view0turn38view3turn18view8turn19view1

**Make the direct recurrent \(V(s)\) head permanent and demote \(Q(s,\mathrm{op})\) to auxiliary status.**  
Core idea: the clean architecture is one recurrent value head for PPO/GAE and one optional auxiliary op-conditioned head for telemetry or side losses. This avoids advantage cancellation and separates the estimate of “how good is this history-state under the current policy” from “how good was this chosen operation conditional on the state”. Required assumptions: \(V(s)\) is easier to learn than \(Q(s,\mathrm{op})\) for your behaviour policy; the recurrent state/history contains the information needed for state value. These assumptions **probably hold**. Expected effect: **large improvement in operation-head signal quality** and **moderate EV stabilisation** beyond the expected-bootstrap patch. Implementation cost: **low-to-medium**. Evidence strength: **strong theoretical grounding; direct empirical evidence in your exact setting is thinner, but the negative case for your current action-conditioned baseline is strong**. Confidence: **likely** beneficial. citeturn39view0turn39view2turn27search3turn27search2

**De-shape the main critic target and give the counterfactual contribution its own stream.**  
Core idea: train the primary \(V\) head on a lower-variance return made of exact PBRS deltas, terminal accuracy, rent, and the smaller Markovian costs. Put the high-CoV leave-one-out contribution into a separate auxiliary head or prediction stream with its own normaliser, so the main EV metric reflects the part of the return that is actually stable enough to regress. Required assumptions: the counterfactual contribution is a noisy *credit-assignment aid*, not an indispensable part of the true task objective; PBRS is exact; escrow clipping is either removed or treated as a separate non-PBRS mechanism. These assumptions **hold only partially**. They hold if your real objective is eventual validation accuracy net cost and the contribution term is merely an attribution surrogate. They do **not** hold if the contribution channel encodes extra preferences you genuinely want in the optimum. Expected effect: **medium-to-large improvement in EV stability** if that term really dominates the target variance. Implementation cost: **medium** because it touches reward bookkeeping, target normalisation, and EV diagnostics. Evidence strength: **strong PBRS theory, moderate reward-decomposition evidence, but direct PPO/recurrent evidence is limited**. Confidence: **likely** beneficial, but with a real exploration-risk caveat. citeturn12search0turn24view0turn24view1turn20view3turn21view11turn30view1

**Add COMA-style per-head counterfactual baselines only where the head is discrete, sparse, and cheap to marginalise.**  
Core idea: for causally active heads, compute a per-head advantage by marginalising that head’s action while holding the realised sibling actions fixed. This is the exact analogue of COMA’s “marginalise one agent, keep the rest fixed” construction. It is most attractive for the top-level operation head and any small-cardinality sub-heads; it is much less attractive for wide or awkward schedule-parameter heads. Required assumptions: you can evaluate headwise counterfactuals cheaply and respect the causal mask. These assumptions **hold for some of your heads, not all**. Expected effect: **small-to-medium improvement** in sparse-head learning signal, more for actor quality than for critic EV. Implementation cost: **medium-to-high** depending on how many heads you support. Evidence strength: **strong in MARL, transfer to single-agent factored PPO is inferential rather than settled**. Confidence: **about as likely as not**, but good enough to rank after the first three steps. citeturn21view9turn21view10turn18view4

**Tighten recurrent and normalisation hygiene around the new critic.**  
Core idea: if any reward channel depends on hidden history state such as escrow windows or clipping memory, expose that state explicitly to the critic; if the auxiliary \(Q\) or contribution heads make the shared recurrent trunk unstable, split the critic recurrence from the actor recurrence; and if you change the regressand, change the normalisation regime accordingly. Required assumptions: the critic currently does not see all reward-relevant state, and PopArt-lite can drift when target composition changes. These assumptions **probably hold**. Expected effect: **small-to-medium**, but important for preventing the other fixes from being blurred by recurrent partial observability or normaliser drift. Implementation cost: **medium**. Evidence strength: **moderate**. Confidence: **likely** beneficial as supporting hygiene, not as the main fix. citeturn32view0turn32view1turn18view7turn34search4turn37view4

## Why the two fixes are complementary

Change B does **not** subsume Change A. If you fix the baseline and bootstrap but keep regressing the critic on a summed reward whose dominant component has a coefficient of variation around the values you reported, the critic still sees a noisy target. Expect more stable and less self-cancelling advantages, but not a fully stable EV. Conversely, Change A does **not** subsume Change B. If you de-shape the target but continue to use \(Q(s,\mathrm{op})\) as both baseline and single-sample bootstrap, the operation head can still suffer from action-conditioned cancellation and the target can still carry policy-sampled next-op variance. The literature lines up with that decomposition: Expected Sarsa / EPG / MAC reduce variance from action sampling, while PBRS and reward decomposition address the value surface itself. Confidence: **high**. citeturn38view0turn18view8turn19view1turn20view3turn30view1

In cost-adjusted terms, the first ablation should be the **expected bootstrap plus state baseline** because it is cheap, theory-backed, and directly addresses a known avoidable variance source. If EV volatility remains high after that change, the posterior probability that the high-CoV contribution term is the main remaining culprit rises substantially. In other words: **B first because it is the cleaner estimator fix; A second because it removes the remaining target-noise floor**. Confidence: **very likely**. citeturn38view0turn38view3turn39view0

## Why the critic-free alternative is not the right primary move

RLOO and GRPO are real alternatives, but the evidence base is mostly in RLHF and reasoning-style sequence optimisation, not dense-reward recurrent control. RLOO uses multiple online samples and a leave-one-out baseline to form an unbiased variance-reduced estimator; GRPO drops the critic and estimates a baseline from group scores, explicitly to save training resources. Recent work even argues that explained variance can tell you when a learned critic hurts more than it helps, and proposes adaptive switching between critic-based and critic-free baselines. Those are useful ideas, but the transfer to your problem is weak because your setting relies on **long-horizon sequential bootstrapping under truncation**, sparse/conditional head activity, and a recurrent latent state. Dropping the critic means giving up the very mechanism that lets PPO/GAE push value information across truncated segments. Confidence: **medium-high** that critic-free should be a diagnostic ablation, not the main plan. citeturn36view0turn36view1turn36view2turn36view4turn29view1

A critic-free method becomes more plausible only if you can generate **multiple matched rollouts from the same conditioning context** cheaply enough that a group-relative baseline is informative, and only if your critic EV is genuinely near-zero or negative for extended periods even after the baseline redesign. That design pattern is natural in prompt-conditioned LLM training, where the same prompt can produce several sampled completions, but it is much less natural in your environment unless you can do paired-seed or matched-host rollouts from identical architecture/training snapshots. Even then, you would still lose the bootstrap that recurrent PPO uses to cope with truncation. Confidence: **medium**. citeturn36view0turn36view2turn29view1

## Traps, diagnostics, and open questions

The main trap in de-shaping is **quietly changing the optimum while thinking PBRS protects you**. PBRS protects you only for the exact potential-difference component. If the leave-one-out contribution was doing more than attribution — for example, encoding intervention timing or magnitude preferences not present in terminal accuracy plus rent — removing it does change the optimisation problem. Likewise, clipped escrow is no longer exact PBRS when the clip binds. The safe interpretation is: de-shaping is optimum-preserving **only if** the remaining sparse reward plus exact PBRS already defines the objective you truly care about. citeturn12search0turn24view0turn30view1

The main trap in the marginal/expected baseline is **policy-coupling across PPO epochs**. If you compute \(V_{\text{marg}}(s)\) using the continually updated current policy during the inner PPO epochs, the target itself moves, which is precisely what your anchored-reference design was trying to avoid. The expectation should therefore be taken under the rollout-time policy or the anchored reference pass, and stored if needed. A second trap is normalisation drift: because your current scheme is PopArt-lite rather than full PopArt, changing the regressand composition can itself create transient instability unless you reset or separate normalisers by stream. citeturn38view0turn37view4turn37view1

The main trap in COMA-style per-head baselines is **overextending them to heads where they do not buy much**. They are most compelling for discrete sparse heads with cheap counterfactual enumeration. For continuously parameterised schedule heads or heads that are active only under another head’s choice, the extra machinery may not justify itself. The literature is strong that counterfactual baselines help in cooperative MARL, but the same literature is also clear that the gains depend on efficient critic structure and correct conditioning. That makes this a third-stage optimisation, not a first-stage one. citeturn21view9turn21view10turn18view4

The right diagnostics are straightforward. After the first change, watch whether the **operation-head advantage variance** and the **critic EV volatility** drop even if median EV does not jump dramatically. After the second, compare EV on the de-shaped \(V\)-target and on a recomposed sum of the separate heads; if the main EV becomes stable while the auxiliary contribution head remains noisy, that is a success, not a failure. If the sparse sub-heads are still weak after both changes, that is when head-specific counterfactual baselines and active-subset per-head standardisation become worth the engineering complexity. On per-head standardisation itself, the literature is thinner than on the other items; it is common PPO/MAPPO hygiene, but not an especially well-settled theory result for factored single-agent action spaces. Confidence in that last point is **medium-low**, and it should be presented as an engineering choice, not a consensus theorem. citeturn18view5turn26view3turn20view7turn18view4

The literature is strong on the first two moves, moderate on reward decomposition, and materially thinner on the exact transfer of COMA-style baselines and headwise normalisation to a recurrent, causally masked, factored-action single-agent PPO. That is the main limitation of the evidence base. The recommendation above therefore separates **high-confidence fixes** from **plausible but more researchy additions**, rather than pretending that all six ideas are equally settled. citeturn39view0turn38view0turn20view3turn21view9
